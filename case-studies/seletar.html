Website redesign UX Research Lead 2025 · 6 weeks

Fast through the gate. Slow on the homepage.

Seletar Airport gets travellers from curb to gate in minutes. Its website didn't move nearly as fast. Over six weeks, I led the research that uncovered why and helped redesign the site to finally match the airport's speed.

Challenge

Seletar Airport's biggest strength is speed. Firefly flights land in Subang, cutting 45–90 minutes off the trip into Kuala Lumpur. The website never said so. It read like a brochure for any airport, buried the few things people needed, and left first-time travellers with nothing to prepare them.

My Contribution

I led the six-week research sprint end to end, scoping the project to what Seletar Airport's own website could fix, running 9 discovery interviews, and facilitating two rounds of usability testing. My findings shaped the navigation, what each version said first, and where the design should go next.

The problem space

A 15-minute airport, with nothing to say about it.

Most flights from Singapore land at Kuala Lumpur International Airport (KLIA) and the airport is over an hour from the city. Firefly's flights from Seletar land at Subang Airport instead, right on the edge of Kuala Lumpur. That one difference saves passengers 45–90 minutes, and travellers happily pay S$50–100 more for it. We call this The Subang Advantage and it's the entire reason these travellers exist.

Despite this, the website looked like a brochure for a generic airport and not a tool built around the one thing travellers actually cared about.

My Call

I scoped this research to what Seletar Airport's own website could fix and not Firefly's booking or flight systems, even though Firefly's Subang route is the entire reason these travellers exist.

The Redesign

From a static brochure to a tool people actually use

We turned each problem into a question, and used the answer to guide what each part of the site needed to do.

New homepage

Homepage with the three primary actions (Flights / Getting Here / Facilities) visible near the top, no scrolling required.

How might we turn the website into a tool that helps people plan, decide, and trust Seletar?

A homepage that gets to the point

Flights, transport, and passenger guide now sit on the homepage itself — what used to take 4–5 clicks now takes 1-2.

Navigation labels changed from brochure-speak ("Airport Guide", "E-Resources") to words people actually look for: "Flights", "Getting Here", "Passenger Guide".

"Getting home" screen + wayfinding map

Transport options screen alongside the simple arrivals-to-pickup walking map.

How might we make every traveller's exit predictable, guided, and stress-free?

A clear way home

A one-tap "Transport" options the moment someone lands — Grab, taxi, and bus, plus what to do if Grab isn't available.

We mapped the walk from arrivals to the pickup point, so the way out is as obvious as the way in.

How might we reduce uncertainty with clear, reassuring information?

Wording that matches what people expect

"Check Status" became "View Flight Schedule" — a small change that removed confusion with buying a ticket, and was one of the clearest wins in testing.

For first-time flyers, a simple visual timeline — check-in, security, boarding, arrival — shows what to expect, in plain language rather than technical detail.

User Testing

Two rounds of testing our prototype, real travellers, honest scores

Round 1 put the new design in front of 5 people in person. Round 2 tested the updated version with 6 people remotely, including one goal that quietly changed shape along the way.

The win

"I appreciate the lack of clutter… it presents the user with what's most likely to be the most important thing."

Without logging in, people found their flight status immediately. "View Flight Schedule". We replaced the wording from Round 1 and it worked exactly as intended.

what still slowed people down, particularly tourists arriving in Singapore

"I don't know what an EZ-Link card is… I would appreciate more information."

#1 Confusing local payment methods

We added payment icons to ease uncertainty but "EZ-Link" (Singapore's local transit card) still confused first-time visitors. The fix for our third iteration was to show familiar, global payment methods first, with local options as a secondary detail.

#2 Food and facilities were hard to find

tucked inside the "Passenger Guide" rather than under an obvious "Facilities" section.

#3 Transport defaulted to "Public Bus" first

fine for some, but jarring for the majority who wanted a taxi or Grab option shown first.

The result

Where the design landed and what’s next

Round 2 pointed at four contained fixes for Version 3: a clearer “Facilities” section, removing the “Public Bus” default, explicit tap-to-pay reassurance for tourists, and small interaction polish on desktop. Version 3 hasn’t been tested yet, but the direction is clear and both rounds showed real, measurable improvement in how quickly people could plan a trip through Seletar Airport.

We shared these findings directly with Seletar Airport, and are still waiting to hear back.

Version 3 prototype — full preview (untested)

A navigational bar that surfaces key services users need and relabelling of navigational labels aligned with users’ existing mental models and reduced cognitive load during scanning, “getting to & from” to “transport”.

Fix 1 — Facilities nav, before / after

Redesigned the layout to optimise viewport real estate, moving critical information above the fold. By consolidating data that previously required extensive scrolling, we drastically reduced the user’s interaction cost and improved immediate data scannability

Fix 2 — Transport default, before / after

Given that most passengers arrive and depart via a ride-hailing service, it now appears first, instead of car/public transport.

Reflections

What this project taught me

01 Research as the anchor

Wording is a UI element

Good copy carries as much weight as any button. “Easy to use” for a global traveller means bridging local terms like “EZ-Link” with what visitors already understand. I learned to prioritise what people need to know over what’s merely nice to know. Solid research is what let a three-person team move fast and stay aligned for six weeks.

02 Defining the audience

Who is “the traveller”, really?

I started by framing our audience broadly “commercial passengers who want speed.” Through research and testing, it became clear the people who needed the most help were specifically first-time travellers navigating an unfamiliar airport. A returning regular and a first-timer have very different needs, and the redesign only worked once we designed for the right one.

03 Scoping the research

Two journeys, one terminal

Seletar Airport and Firefly are two separate organisations sharing one terminal experience. Early on, I drew a deliberate line: this research would cover what Seletar Airport’s website could own and fix (not Firefly’s booking or flight systems) even though Firefly’s Subang route is the entire reason travellers are there. Keeping that line clear from day one kept a small team focused on what we could ship in six weeks.
