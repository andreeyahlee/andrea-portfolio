From rejected dashboard to validated redesign.

ARVO is an AI tool that helps managers spot early signs of team burnout and suggests what to do next. Managers rejected the first version. I led the team that found out why and discovered most of the problems weren't ones design could fix.

Challenge

Assessing all seven issues, most weren't things a redesign could fix on its own. The real challenge was figuring out how to rebuild ARVO around how managers actually work, not just how it looked, and knowing which problems were ours to solve and which belonged to the product itself.

Impact

Two issues where (1) managers were time-poor to read the dashboard and (2) too overloaded to spot what mattered, were solved with a clearer layout and a simple visual signal. Another two deeper ones, around trust and powerlessness, went back to the founder with the design implications explained. The remaining three, confusing AI language was rewritten from workshop terms; accessibility and a redesigned task step await round-two results.

My Contribution

I led the full six-week sprint from start to finish, covering scoping, recruitment, facilitation and synthesis. I designed and ran the workshop myself, bringing the founder in as a collaborator rather than someone we reported back to, so the decisions carried her ownership from day one. When research surfaced seven problems pulling in different directions, I took lead in deciding what ARVO should focus on first.

The Redesign

The two problems we could solve.

A single page replaced a dense, multi-tab dashboard.

Managers had no time to dig through multiple tabs to piece data together. We rebuilt ARVO as one screen where everything in a single glance, no navigation. This solved the Manager's Paradox: the tool now fits the ninety seconds a manager actually has.

Traffic-light dials replaced a wall of equal-weight data.

Cognitive overload is what happens when everything competes for attention at once. On the old screen every number and walls of text looked equally urgent, so nothing stood out. I added a simple red, amber, green system, so a manager sees at a glance what needs them and what's fine. The screen finally had a hierarchy.

Knowing what not to touch

The hardest call was leaving something alone.

The brightspot protected

Energisers & Drainers

All 12 managers responded positively to this feature and it mirrored how they already think about their teams. The instinct on a redesign is to change everything. However, a principle we applied was if it ain't broke, don't change it. Protecting what works is as much a design decision as fixing what doesn't.

Reflections

What I learned as Lead while working on ARVO.

Question the brief on day one.

The most useful thing I did wasn't jumping straight into redesigning a screen with the team. It was asking whether the screens were even the problem. Next time that question comes first.

Handing problems back is a deliverable.

An eye-opening experience was getting used to the possibility that design can have its limitations while trying to solve issues users may have. Through ARVO, a written brief naming each returned problem, its owner, and suggesting next step was just as useful to ARVO's Founder than any feature built on a whim.

Protecting what works is a decision.

Considering whether to leave Energisers & Drainers untouched took more conviction than redesigning it. Restraint is a skill I want to keep practising.

Alignment before the client, every time.

I've always believed that presenting a united front is key to giving a client peace of mind, so I made it a point to get my team fully aligned before every meeting with our Client. She actually called this out as a major strength in her feedback. Seeing the impact it had, I now do this intentionally with every project.
